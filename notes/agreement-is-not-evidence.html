Evals

My LLM judge agreed with me 95% of the time. It proved nothing.

I labelled 70 replies by hand to check an AI judge. The first 40 gave 95% agreement and a kappa of 0.00. A second round, built to contain failures, got to 0.29. What both rounds taught me.

October 15, 2026 · 4 min read · Evals

The setup

My third portfolio project is an evaluation harness for two AI assistants I built for a fictional hotel group: one answers front-desk staff's policy questions, the other drafts replies to guest emails. Most of what it checks is deterministic: the right fee, every number traceable to a source, the email about an injury sent to a manager. The hotel, guests and documents are synthetic.

Some things code can't check: is the reply complete, is the tone right, is it clear? For those I added an LLM judge with a versioned rubric, and set one rule before writing any of it: a judge metric only counts if it agrees with my hand labels beyond chance, measured by Cohen's kappa of at least 0.6. Even then it never gates a release.

Round 1: 95% agreement, kappa 0.00

I sampled 40 replies, about half from outputs a deterministic check had flagged, and labelled them blind. I passed all 40. The judge passed 38.

That's 95% agreement, and a kappa of 0.00. Kappa asks how much better than chance two raters agree. When both of us say "pass" nearly every time, chance agreement is already 95%; a judge that never reads the reply would score the same. My sample had nothing to disagree about. The flagged outputs had wrong facts, not bad writing.

Round 2: built to contain failures

So I built a second blind set of 30: 15 real replies the judge had failed on full runs, and 15 real replies I degraded by hand. I made some robotic, some cold, some rambling, filled some with internal jargon, and dropped one of the guest's requests from others. The two halves were shuffled and looked the same on the labeling page; only hidden metadata said which was which.

I failed 6 of the 30. The judge failed all 30.

On all 70 labels, kappa was 0.22 with the first rubric and 0.20 with the second. The judge caught every reply I failed, but it also failed most of the ones I passed. Round 2 on its own scored close to zero, and because it was built to be full of failures, that number isn't what everyday traffic would give.

One more revision, then stop

I allowed myself two rubric revisions per round, scored on the recorded replies with no new labels. The third version raised the bar for "fail" to something a reader would actually feel: confused, misled, left without an answer, or put off. Style imperfections pass.

Kappa on all 70 rose to 0.29. Tone reached 0.51, clarity 0.23, completeness 0.00. Nothing crossed 0.6, so nothing is trusted, nothing is reported as a judge score, and the release gates stay deterministic.

I didn't make a fourth version. Tone was the only quality close to the line, and nudging it over on the same 70 labels it was revised on would mean fitting the rubric to my answers, not checking the judge.

The uncomfortable part

I passed 11 of the 15 replies I had degraded myself. A curt reply about a guest's noisy room was a fail; a mechanical one about a pet policy was a pass. The judge failed every one of those replies. Where its line and mine differ isn't written down anywhere: neither of us had a definition of "fail" beyond one sentence per quality.

So the low kappa isn't only the judge's problem. Calibration needs a written standard first: a few pass and fail examples for each quality, ideally agreed by two people. How often two people agree is the ceiling for any judge.

What I'd tell myself at the start

  • Report kappa next to agreement, always. 95% agreement and a kappa of 0.00 described the same 40 labels.
  • Put failures in the calibration set on purpose, and say so every time you show its kappa.
  • Write the standard before you label. Otherwise you're calibrating the judge against a mood.
  • Stop revising when you start fitting. A rubric tuned to the labels it is scored on proves nothing new.

The deterministic gates did their job in the same project: a change that sent two passages instead of five to save tokens was caught, naming the two questions it broke. The judge is the part that still needs better evidence before it counts.

Everything here is in the project's repository: all 70 labels, the three rubric versions, the judge's verdicts and the calibration numbers.
