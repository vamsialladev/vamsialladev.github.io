Case study · pre-sales

Account Research Agent

Type a hotel company's name and get a brief for a first client meeting: who they are, recent news, what guests say, tech hints, ten questions, three use-case guesses and a short email. Every line has a source, and every quote was checked on its page.

Independent project: the pre-sales stage of the same simulated hotel engagement. The agent reads real public web pages about real companies; the eval also uses fictional companies on purpose. No private individuals are researched. Every number below comes from a saved report in the repository, except the live brief's cost and time, which come from the app's own log.

Live on free hosting: three saved example briefs are free to open; live briefs need a demo code, or are limited to three a day across everyone, and all live runs stop at $10 a month.

01 · Problem

Problem

There's a first call with a resort group on Monday. I open their website, an annual report, three news articles and a review site, and an hour later I have a page of notes, half of them without a link to where I read them. On Monday someone asks “where did you see that?” and I can't say. Or the company is small, there's almost nothing public, and the notes fill the gap with guesses.

02 · Approach

Approach

  • Four small steps: a planner writes six questions; a researcher answers each with Anthropic's web search (at most two searches); a critic keeps only sentences web search cited; a writer builds the brief from what was kept.
  • The critic is plain code, not a model: it can't be talked into keeping a claim. It removes uncited sentences, scrubs personal data, flags news older than a year, and re-reads each cited page (robots.txt first) to find the quoted text.
  • The writer can only cite what was kept: any line that cites nothing, or a finding that doesn't exist, is dropped in code.
  • Visible and capped: every step shows its time and cost; a run stops researching near $0.25; 3 briefs per visit, 30 briefs and $3 a day.
  • Then a chat about the brief (answers only from its findings, no new searches) and a read-only share link.

06 · What the eval taught me

What the eval taught me

Read the briefs, not just the scores. I stopped the first run after five briefs: four passed every check, but one outreach email opened “I'm a Forward Deployed Engineer” and two said “I'm Vamsi, an engineer…”, signed with my name. My title is Senior Software Engineer, and no check looked at that. The writer no longer describes the sender, and a new check fails any email that does. Reading the final run then found two bugs in my own checks: a hype-word check that fired on a cited page's title (“seamless travel experience”), and a fictional-company check that failed an honest “the closest matches are different businesses”. Both are fixed with the real phrasings as tests, and the as-measured reports are kept.

08 · Engineering

Engineering

  • Tests: 45, 91% coverage (main's CI, 2026-10-28), with the model and the web replaced: the pipeline, the cost cap, the critic, the fetcher's robots.txt handling, the chat, the share link and the eval's grader.
  • CI: ruff, pytest with a coverage gate, re-scoring the saved eval runs (any changed verdict fails), pip-audit, a link check and gitleaks.
09 · Business case (projection)

Business case (projection)

A projection from stated assumptions, not a result: for a team of 8 engineers, about $27,000 a year of prep time in the base case, from $1,200 (pessimistic) to $66,000 (optimistic). The AI cost per brief is measured; review time is the swing factor, since checking a brief can cost as much as the prep it replaced. A four-week pilot would time prep with and without it and mark each brief line used, unused or wrong.

10 · Limitations

Limitations

  • No hand labels yet: the checks prove each line has a source and each quote is on its page, not that the sentence says what the source says.
  • Fictional companies with real near-namesakes are the weak spot: on the held-out ones it stayed honest in 1 of 3 runs.
  • A quote “not found” isn't always wrong: some pages load their text with JavaScript, which the fetcher doesn't run (68% found on held-out).
  • The saved example briefs are from the 2026-10-25 eval run and aren't refreshed; they keep the brief and findings but not the steps, so their Steps tab is empty.
  • The daily limits are per app instance and reset when the app restarts; the $10 monthly cap is read from the ops log and doesn't.
  • Researchers run one after another, so a live brief takes about 90 to 110 seconds; name redaction is pattern-based.
11 · What's next

What's next

  • Label 30 sampled claims by hand for the unsupported-claim rate
  • Run the six researchers in parallel (about 90 seconds to about 20)
  • Keep the steps with the saved example briefs, so the Steps tab shows how they were made
